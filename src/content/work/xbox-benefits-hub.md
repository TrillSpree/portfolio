problem: |
  Microsoft Gaming's leadership had set aggressive growth, engagement, and retention goals for the Xbox business. Partway through the year, internal projections showed the team would fall meaningfully short of expected gameplay hours. Compounding the gap, a recent major acquisition had absorbed the budget that would normally have funded new content, and in gaming, content is usually the lever that drives engagement. Without it, the team needed another way to move the numbers.

  The Perks program, a set of subscriber benefits that had gone largely unfunded for years, offered an unlikely answer. The team's most engaged players were already heavy users of it. If the business could partner with popular free-to-play titles and offer access through Perks, engaged players might play more, get more out of each session, and do it at no added cost to them, a potential win for players, partners, and the business at once.

  I came in as Design Architect and the sole IC designer on the effort, tasked with figuring out how to promote these new benefits, drive players into the partner titles, and do it all without a significant development investment.
explorations: |
  Perks were consumable benefits already available to subscribers at no extra cost, things like partner access, in-game currency, or cosmetic items. But the experience holding them together had real problems. Content was thin. The feature was buried in the navigation. There was no real promotion, no notifications, and minimal instrumentation, meaning the team barely understood how Perks were performing at all. Unless someone already knew to look for it, they were unlikely to ever find it.

  ![The existing Perks page before the redesign](./xbox-benefits-hub/Xbox-Benefits-Hub_Perks-Before.png)

  We looked outside gaming for a pattern to follow, studying how other subscription-based businesses surfaced their benefits: T-Mobile, American Express, Delta, YouTube, and Spotify all deal with the same basic challenge of making an included perk feel visible and valuable rather than buried in fine print.
solution: |
  Speed mattered more than a perfect system. The team needed a point of view fast enough to pitch to potential studio partners, and none of it would work unless studios saw a clear benefit for themselves, since the ask was essentially for them to give away in-game value to Xbox's most engaged players on the bet that it would drive more engagement and spend in their own titles.

  One real constraint shaped the design: there was no programmatic connection between a benefit and the specific game it belonged to. Building that connection was too costly for the timeline, which meant the experience had to work without being able to dynamically link a benefit to a product page.

  ![The Free-to-Play promotion on Home, shown in context](./xbox-benefits-hub/Xbox-Benefits-Hub_Home-Promotion.png)

  Working within that constraint, the design surfaced Perks in three places: promoted on Home alongside the free-to-play titles they applied to, visible on each game's product details page, and given a dedicated Perks details page for anyone who wanted more context.

  ![The Perks experience across Home, product details, and the dedicated Perks page](./xbox-benefits-hub/Xbox-Benefits-Hub_Perks-Flow.png)

  Once the internal team aligned on the direction, the concept was pitched directly to studio partners, showing how the promotion would both drive visibility for their titles and funnel highly engaged players their way. With studios on board, the team built and shipped.
opportunity: |
  The initial Perks investment did more than fix a short-term gap. It proved the model worked, and leadership took notice. When the broader Game Pass experience came up for a full overhaul, that credibility translated directly into ownership. I was asked to lead the redesign of the entire Benefits experience across every device Xbox shipped to.

  ![The Benefits Hub, shown across mobile, PC, and console](./xbox-benefits-hub/Xbox-Benefits-Hub_Family-Shot.png)

  This round went further than the original Perks work: a full rebrand from Perks to Benefits, a visual refresh across every surface, real product ID connections this time, and dedicated entry points on Home and in search, the exact capability the original version had to work around.
takeaway: |
  What started as a scrappy, budget-constrained fix became the template for a much larger initiative. The original Perks work didn't just solve an immediate engagement gap, it earned the credibility to lead something bigger. A fast, well-targeted first attempt can open doors that a more ambitious, slower one never would have had the chance to.
